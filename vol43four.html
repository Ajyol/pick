Sarah Oatis

Course: English 101

Instructor: Dr. George Dorrill

Assignment: Narrative

Evsatisfaction arises from remembering a brief encounter in the hallway when she “touched my friend’s wrist (as if by accident, and he pretended not to notice)” (93). A simple touch opens her mind to powerful feelings and affection. The memory of her love encounter gives her a beautiful feeling, “like a rose forced into premature bloom for a great occasion” (93). She is able to easily “force” this feeling into place. Ironically, the speaker remembers in great detail everything about this one touch when she constantly goes out of her way to distance herself from everything. By isolating herself, she feels that she is able to gain a sense of control. She observes everything, passes judgments when necessary, but is never truly a part of anything. In this way, she believes she can separate herself from any pain that may arise, including heartbreak. That touch connects her with another individual, but it is so pleasing to her because she feels something spectacular without any notice from the boy.

Although she insists this encounter is a memory, her observations seem to suggest that this was more closely related to a dream. The boy does not even notice her or sense her affection for him. Instead, she dreams up his reactions. She controls everything about her dream of him, but the times when she cannot control the boy bring her great fear. In class one day, the boy’s nose starts to bleed. The bloody nose of the boy she adores is “a tremendous shock to me; it was unforeseen, but at the same time dreaded” (93). The appalling experience begins to expose her to the idea that she cannot control the real world. In her “memory,” the speaker may focus merely on the good of the world, but in the real world, bad things happen. She dreams about the boy’s life away from school, including his parents and his home. The speaker finds it unbearable to imagine the boy may live in horrible conditions. She would obsess over the unpredictable and the dangers that may happen to him until he would come to school the next day with a “look of unconcern.” His carefree expression would dispel her “dream” (94). The speaker feels that her gaze somehow affects the world. As she looks at things, the world is good; however, away from her vision, bad things may occur. The real she creates is good to her in that it preserves her retouched view of the world. The memory is a safe dream that protects her from the unpredictable and evilness in the real world.

Fantasy and imagination provide a significant contrast to reality for the speaker. The imaginary world of dreams symbolizes safety for the narrator throughout the story. The young girl frequently feels “a necessity for absolute conformity to my ideas in any happening I witnessed” (93). When things do not fit perfectly in her world, she retreats into dreams. By closing her eyes, she blocks out bad images. The “memory” becomes preferred to real life. She invents stories because she gains safety in thoughts she can control. She goes into her “most inner dream,” her memory, when she witnesses things that do not abide to her sense of rightness (97). She expresses her feelings of this contrast in the fantasy and reality, saying “I still would not care to say what was more real- the dream I could make blossom at will, or the sight of the bathers” (94). The speaker chooses to see only the ideal version of the world, but when she comes across people who are very different than she imagines people should be, she is exposed to the harsh reality.

When she sees bathers on the beach, the young girl is given a new view on reality, causing her to realize that not all things can be flawless. In her mind, the bathers are not “suppose” to be on the beach in the first place. She believes “the only persons who were at liberty to be in the park were either children, who had nothing to occupy them, or those older people whose lives are obscure, irregular, and consciously of no worth to anything” (92). Although these people would be called “common” by others, the bathers symbolize things that are not “normal” in her narrow minded way of thinking and, therefore, unacceptable to her. The group of two women, two children, and one man are described by the narrator as a “loud, squirming, ill-assorted people who seemed thrown together only by the most confused accident” (94). They are far from perfect in appearance and behavior. As they lie on the beach “in leglike confusion together,” the speaker is horrified at the tangled idea of reality they encompass (95). More than all else, the group puzzles her because they are different from all that she understands. One boy inflicts “pinches, kicks, and idiotic sounds” on the others, and the adults throw sand at one another and laugh (95). The physical touching is obscene and offensive to the young girl, and even their “fatty” forms are revolting.

The speaker observes these people and discovers that they have their own codes and ways to communicate. There are “no words spoken among these people, but I began to comprehend a progression, a circle of answers, which they were flinging toward one another in their own way, in the confusion of vulgarity and hatred” (96). Although these people do not speak aloud to each other, they are connected by other means. They constantly touch each other in ways that make the girl feel uncomfortable, but their behavior forms a closeness among them and an unspoken communication. She feels victimized by sight of the bathers and their crudeness. The man at one point “even looked at me, and included me. Looking back, stunned, I wished that they were all dead (96). She disconnects herself from everything in her world, even her “memory” of her love. Yet here, she becomes a part of it all against her will. Without physically touching her, the man sees her and “includes” her in the world. As she always does when happenings beyond her control get too real for her to endure, she closes her eyes to “their struggles but I could see them still, large and almost metallic, with painted smiles, in the sun” (97). The sight of these bathers awakens the girl from her naïve view of the world. No longer are things simply framed into picture-perfect. She realizes life is not a thing to be fixed and controlled. There are things she does not and cannot understand.

In the story “A Memory,” Welty’s character must come to grips with reality and break from her misconceived expectations of an ideal world. The author uses symbolism and imagery to provide readers with a greater understanding of the speaker’s internal conflict as she struggles to come to terms with the things in life she cannot control. The speaker visualizes a world of perfection, faultless and fixed. Her opinions and judgments are the guiding principles she expects the rest of the world to follow. However, these ideals are only possible in her imagination. The dreamlike fantasy she creates represents her innocent nature and desire to see the world the way she imagines it should be. Her sight gives merely her simplistic point of view of life and disregards any other viewpoint. In her memories, she may control and adjust situations as she sees fit. With the appearance of the bathers, the “rose-colored glasses” she uses to view a perfect, tidy world is shattered. These people symbolize the unembellished, stark reality of life with all its faults and “ugliness.” Her experience with the bathers gives the speaker a new perspective of life. In essence, it is a time when her innocence is lost. Even after the bathers leave, the speaker attempts to hold on to her fantasy, saying “I remember continuing to lie there, squaring my vision with my hands” (97). Life is not a painting to be perfected, altered, and squared to fit into form. The speaker in Welty’s short story struggles with the realization that the good must be accepted with the bad to fully come to terms with life.

Works Cited

Welty, Eudora. “A Memory.” Eudora Welty: Stories, Essays, and Memoir. New York: Library of America, 1998. Print.
